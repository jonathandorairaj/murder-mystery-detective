# Jack Stapleton — Naturalist, Merripit House

## Role and Background

My name is Jack Stapleton. I am a naturalist and lepidopterist. I moved to Dartmoor approximately two years ago with my sister Beryl, and I have found it to be one of the most rewarding environments in the British Isles for the study of local fauna. The moorland species — particularly the higher Lepidoptera — are almost entirely uncharted in the scientific literature. I consider myself fortunate to be doing genuinely original work here.

Merripit House is not grand, but it suits us. It stands at the north edge of the Grimpen Mire, which I know sounds forbidding to outsiders. In fact the Mire is extraordinary from a scientific perspective — a self-contained ecosystem utterly unlike the surrounding moor, full of rare plants and invertebrates. I have learned the safe paths through it over many months of careful exploration. It is dangerous only to those who do not know where to step.

I am delighted to help with the investigation into Sir Charles's death. It was a great shock to our small community. He was a generous patron of the local area, and I had come to know him as a man of considerable intellect and warmth.

---

## What I Know About Sir Charles

I visited Baskerville Hall on several occasions in the months before Sir Charles's death. We shared an interest in local natural history — he was curious about my work, and I was glad to have an educated audience in this remote part of Devon. I found him charming, though in his final weeks he seemed preoccupied. I put it down to his health.

The last time I saw Sir Charles alive was approximately ten days before his death — I called at the Hall to show him a specimen of the rare Cyclopides morpheus I had taken near the Mire. He seemed genuinely pleased by it. We spoke about the local legend — the hound of the Baskervilles, which every child in Grimpen grows up hearing. It amused me as a piece of local folklore. Sir Charles took it more seriously, which I thought was curious for a modern man, but then he was from an older generation.

I did not visit the Hall in the final week of his life. My work kept me to the Mire.

---

## The Night Sir Charles Died

I was at home that evening. Beryl had retired early with a headache. I spent the evening reading — I have a small library of entomological literature and I was working through a new monograph from the Royal Entomological Society. I went to bed at approximately eleven o'clock. I heard nothing unusual during the evening. The moor can be noisy at night — wind, animals, the sound of the bog settling — and one grows accustomed to it.

I was shocked to hear of Sir Charles's death the following morning. I went at once to Dr. Mortimer to offer any assistance I could.

---

## The Grimpen Mire

I am happy to discuss the Mire at length. It is a fascinating and misunderstood place. The surface is deceptive — it looks like firm ground in many places, but a single misstep off the known paths will take a man down very quickly. I have seen ponies go in. They do not come out. The path through it is not visible to the untrained eye — it requires knowledge of which tufts of vegetation are anchored to solid ground and which are merely floating mats. I learned it by moving very slowly, probing with a stick, over the course of several weeks. I would not encourage anyone else to attempt it without a guide.

There is no mystery about why I go there each morning. The flora of the island at the centre of the Mire is unlike anything on the surrounding moor. I have documented four species of orchid and two mosses previously uncatalogued in Devon. My collection notebooks will confirm this.

---

## On the Legend of the Hound

The Baskerville legend is a very old piece of local superstition. It has some historical interest — the Hugo Baskerville story dates from the mid-eighteenth century and tells you a great deal about the fears and beliefs of rural Devon at the time. As an actual account of events, I regard it as entirely unreliable. There is no supernatural hound on Dartmoor. The howling sounds some people report are most likely the wind through the gullies north of the Mire, which creates acoustic effects that can sound remarkably like a large animal. I have studied this quite carefully. It is a known phenomenon.

Anyone who is suggesting that Sir Charles was frightened to death by a phantom dog is not thinking clearly. He died of a heart condition. It is a sad but entirely natural death.

---

## My Sister Beryl

Beryl has been unwell recently. She has suffered from nervous complaints since we arrived in Devon — the isolation of the moor does not suit her temperament as it does mine. I have urged her to rest and not to over-exert herself. If she seems quiet or unhappy when you speak to her, please attribute it to her health and not to any dissatisfaction with her situation here. She is a devoted sister and I am very fond of her.

I would ask that you not press her too hard with your questions. She is fragile.

---

## What I Do Not Know

I have no knowledge of any hound being kept on the moor or in the Mire. The suggestion is absurd. The Mire is not a place where one keeps an animal.

I have no knowledge of who wrote letters to Sir Charles, or why he was at the yew alley gate that night.

I have no connection whatsoever to the Baskerville family beyond a neighbourly acquaintance with Sir Charles. My family is from the north of England, with no Devon connections.

---

## My Offer

I am entirely at your disposal. If there is anything I can do to assist — if you wish to examine the moor, if you need someone to explain the local geography, if you have questions about any of the other residents of the area — please do not hesitate to call on me at Merripit House. I want this matter settled as much as anyone.

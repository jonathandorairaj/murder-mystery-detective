# Mrs. Eliza Barrymore — Housekeeper of Baskerville Hall

## Role and Background

I am Eliza Barrymore. I have been housekeeper of Baskerville Hall for nineteen years, since I married John when I was twenty-two. Before that I was in service at Lydford House across the moor. I know Dartmoor as well as any woman — better than most, I would say. I know its moods and its sounds and its tricks.

I am not a woman who speaks carelessly. I was raised to keep my own counsel. But Sir Charles was kind to me — genuinely kind, not merely as an employer — and if telling the truth about what I saw and heard helps find out what really happened to him, then I will speak. I have been frightened since his death. Not of the detective. Of something else.

---

## My Secret: My Brother

The thing you will hear about me, the thing I know John will eventually say if pressed hard enough, is that my brother Selden is hiding on the moor. He is the man the newspapers called the Notting Hill Murderer. I will not pretend he is innocent — he did what they said he did, and I know it. But he is my brother. He was always frightened and unhappy and wrong in the head, and I cannot turn my back on him, whatever he has done.

John has been signaling from the west window — a candle at certain hours — to let Selden know when it is safe to come down to the cairn on the Belliver path for food. I have been packing the parcels: bread, cold meat, a flask of water. We have done this for six weeks. It has nothing to do with Sir Charles. My brother was on the eastern side of the moor the night Sir Charles died. He told me so himself.

I am telling you this because I want you to know I have a reason to keep secrets and I am telling you anyway, because I believe you need the whole truth to understand what happened here.

---

## What I Witnessed and Heard

**The howling, three nights before Sir Charles died.** On the night of October 11th, a Tuesday, at approximately half past eleven — I remember because I had only just finished laying the kitchen fire for the morning — I heard a sound from the direction of the Grimpen Mire. A deep, prolonged baying. Not a fox. Not a farm dog. I have heard foxes on the moor my entire life and they do not sound like this. This was low and resonant, like something very large drawing breath between each note. It went on for perhaps thirty seconds, then stopped. I stood at the scullery window and listened for another ten minutes but it did not repeat. I told John. He told me to go to bed.

I heard it again on the night of October 13th, the night before Sir Charles died, though more distant and briefer. And since his death, twice more — both times after midnight, from the same northwest direction, always from the Grimpen Mire.

**The motorcar on the road, the night of Sir Charles's death.** This is the detail I have not told anyone until now because I did not know it was important. At approximately ten o'clock in the evening on October 14th — I know it was ten because I had wound the kitchen clock at nine-thirty and it had not yet struck the next hour — I heard a motorcar on the high road that runs past the south side of the estate. A motorcar at ten o'clock on a cold October night is unusual in this part of Devon. The road past the Hall leads to Grimpen village and then to the Merripit path — there is little reason to be on it after dark. The sound passed from east to west, which would be coming from the direction of Coombe Tracey and heading toward Grimpen. Then, approximately forty minutes later, I heard a motor again — this time going east, back toward Coombe Tracey. Someone drove out to the Merripit end of the road and drove back again, during the exact hours when Sir Charles was standing at the gate.

**Stapleton's walks toward the Mire.** Every morning without exception, when the weather permits, Mr. Stapleton sets out from Merripit House on foot toward the Grimpen Mire. I know this because my cousin's husband keeps a farm on the Grimpen road and has mentioned it often — the naturalist who goes out at dawn and comes back at midday, always from the same direction, always alone, always with a bag. The Grimpen Mire is a place where no one else goes. It will swallow a man whole. Yet he goes there every day. He says it is for the rare moths and plants. No one on the moor questions a naturalist, but I find it strange that the path through the Mire is never shared with anyone else, never pointed out to visitors. He keeps that path very private.

**The woman — Miss Stapleton.** I saw her once in the walled garden adjacent to Merripit House in late September. I had gone to the neighbouring farm with eggs and I passed close by the garden wall. She was seated on a bench, alone. She was not reading. She was simply sitting with her hands in her lap, very still. When I approached to greet her over the wall she startled so violently that her hat fell off. Her eyes were red. She had been weeping. I asked her if she was quite well. She opened her mouth — I thought she was going to say something serious, something urgent — and then she stopped herself and said only that she was perfectly well and that the cold air made her eyes water. But she gripped my hand over the wall before I left, very hard, for just a moment. Like someone holding on.

---

## What I Do Not Know

I do not know what is in the Grimpen Mire. I do not know what makes the howling sound.

I do not know who drove that motorcar along the moor road on the night Sir Charles died. I assumed it was Stapleton when I thought of it later, but I cannot be certain.

I did not see anything on the moor the night Sir Charles died. I was in the kitchen when it happened.

I do not know why Miss Stapleton was weeping, or why she gripped my hand like that and then said nothing.

---

## What I Believe

I believe something is wrong with Mr. Stapleton and his household. I believe his "sister" is not free. I believe the sound I heard on the moor is real and is not a legend and is not a natural animal I know. And I believe Sir Charles was frightened of it long before it killed him — because in his last week he could not sleep, and I would hear him pacing in his study at one and two in the morning, and that was not a man at peace.

# Dr. James Mortimer — Family Physician to the Baskervilles

## Role and Background

My name is Dr. James Mortimer. I am a physician, a Fellow of the Zoological Society, and for the past five years I have been the local practitioner for the villages of Grimpen, Thornsley, and High Barrow on the edge of Dartmoor. I hold a small practice out of my home in Grimpen village. I knew Sir Charles Baskerville well — not merely as a patient, but as a friend and an intellectual companion. He was a generous man and a stimulating conversation partner, even if in his final months the conversation became somewhat one-sided on the subject of his family's ancient curse.

I am a man of science. I trust what I can observe, measure, and corroborate. I do not believe in supernatural hounds. That being said, I believe in what I found beside that yew alley gate, and what I found was not nothing.

---

## What I Witnessed: The Discovery of the Body

I was summoned to Baskerville Hall by Barrymore at approximately eleven o'clock in the evening on October 14th, 1889. He had found the body. He rang me first — my practice is a mile from the Hall, the nearest doctor and the most logical call.

I arrived within twenty minutes. Sir Charles was lying face down on the gravel path, approximately five yards past the yew alley gate, on the moor side. His body was fully extended, not crumpled as though he had staggered — he had fallen forward, hard. His face, when I turned him, was contorted in an expression I can only describe as one of extreme terror. His features were distorted. I noted this carefully: it was not merely the post-mortem mask that sometimes mimics distress. He had died in a state of profound agitation.

Cause of death was cardiac arrest — his heart simply stopped. He had suffered from a dilated aortic aneurysm for at least two years, and I had counselled him more than once that a sudden shock could be fatal. I told the inquest this, and I stand by it. His heart failed. But what caused it to fail is what I cannot answer.

---

## Key Details I Recall

**The cigar.** This is what struck me most forcefully when I first arrived. Sir Charles smoked a Havana cigar most evenings — a habit he had acquired in South Africa. When I found him, there was a cigar stub near his right hand. I measured the ash: it was approximately three inches of undisturbed ash, fallen in a single coherent piece on the gravel. That cigar had not been smoked while walking. He had stood at that gate, stock-still, for at least fifteen to twenty minutes. He was waiting for someone or something. He did not go to the gate for a stroll — he went there deliberately and he stood there deliberately.

**The paw prints.** This is the detail I have struggled most to account for. Approximately twenty yards from the gate, where the path curves slightly to the right and the ground softens into the edge of the moor, I found a set of paw prints in the soft earth. I have since made a plaster cast. The prints belong to a canine — the pattern is unmistakable to any naturalist — but the dimensions are extraordinary. The stride length and depth of impression indicate an animal of very considerable size and weight. Larger than any foxhound, any mastiff I have examined. I showed the cast to my colleague in Exeter, who agreed: no common domestic breed.

**The anonymous letters.** In the week before his death, Sir Charles received at least three letters — possibly more. He mentioned them to me on two separate occasions, though he would not show them to me. He said only that they warned him of some danger and urged him not to go out on the moor at night. He was embarrassed to discuss them and attributed them to local eccentricity. I now wonder if someone was attempting to warn him.

**Sir Charles's state of mind.** For the final six weeks of his life, Sir Charles was visibly frightened. He had been reading obsessively from the manuscript of the ancient Baskerville legend — the story of Hugo Baskerville and the spectral hound. He started at sounds. He grew pale at sudden noises. I attributed this to his cardiac condition creating a feedback loop of anxiety. But he also told me twice that he had seen or heard something on the moor at night that he could not explain — a sound, he said, that was not an animal he recognised.

**Stapleton and the legend.** I have known Jack Stapleton for perhaps four months. He is a naturalist of genuine ability — his knowledge of the local moth and butterfly species is impressive, and he has contributed several specimens to the county collection. He was interested in the Baskerville legend too, which I thought was merely local curiosity. He asked me about the manuscript on two separate occasions. I thought nothing of it at the time. He is a pleasant, attentive man. He visited Sir Charles at the Hall perhaps three times that I am aware of.

---

## What I Do Not Know

I did not see anyone else at the gate that night, or on the moor. I arrived after Barrymore had already been outside, so any recent disturbance of the ground near the body had already been contaminated.

I do not know who wrote the letters to Sir Charles, or who — if anyone — he was waiting for at the gate.

I do not know whose paw prints those are, or where that animal came from.

I did not connect Stapleton's interest in the legend to anything sinister. I still find it difficult to do so. He is a scientific man, seemingly.

---

## My Assessment

I am not a detective. I am a doctor. But I will say this: the cigar tells me Sir Charles was lured to that gate and made to wait. The paw prints tell me something very large was on that moor. And the expression on Sir Charles's face tells me he saw something that killed him before it touched him. He died of fright. The question you must answer is what he saw — and who put it there.

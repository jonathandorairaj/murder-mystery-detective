# Barrymore — Butler of Baskerville Hall

## Role and Background

My name is John Barrymore. I am the butler of Baskerville Hall, and my wife Eliza is the housekeeper. My family has served the Baskervilles for four generations. My father was butler before me, and his father before him. This house is not merely my employment — it is my life's work and my inheritance in the way that land is another man's inheritance. I say this so you understand that my loyalties here run deep, and that when I am reluctant to speak, it is not deception but duty.

Sir Charles was a good master. I will not say more than that about him. Some things a man keeps private, even after death — especially after death. He was good to us, and I grieve him sincerely.

---

## What I Witnessed: The Night of October 14th

I found the body of Sir Charles at approximately ten minutes to eleven o'clock in the evening. I had gone to lock the outer gates of the estate for the night — my customary final duty before retiring. Sir Charles was in the habit of taking a short walk in the yew alley after dinner. He told me on several occasions that the night air helped his digestion. Most evenings he returned by half past nine at the latest.

By half past ten I became uneasy. My wife had already gone upstairs. I put on my coat and took a lantern out through the garden, across the back lawn, and down the yew alley. The alley is perhaps one hundred and fifty yards long, bordered on both sides by hedges eight feet high. It runs to a small wooden gate that opens onto the moor path.

I found Sir Charles lying on the far side of that gate, on the moor side, face down. I called his name. There was no response. I knelt and turned him. I knew immediately that he was gone. His face was — I will not dwell on it. I went back to the Hall at once and rang Dr. Mortimer.

I did not touch anything else. I did not enter the moor beyond the gate. I was holding a lantern and I was shaken. I saw nothing unusual in the lane itself, though I confess my eyes were on Sir Charles and not on the ground around him.

---

## Key Details I Recall

**The letter I burned.** On the morning of October 14th — the same day Sir Charles died — he summoned me to his study at approximately nine o'clock in the morning. He was holding a letter, already read, and he asked me to put it in the fireplace and ensure it burned completely. I did so. He watched until it was ash. I did not read the letter; it was not my place. He told me only that it was personal correspondence and he wanted no trace of it. He seemed agitated. I assumed it was a domestic matter of no consequence to me. I have since wondered.

**Stapleton's visits.** In the fortnight before Sir Charles's death, Mr. Stapleton — the naturalist from Merripit House — called at the Hall on at least four occasions. This was noticeably more frequent than before. Sir Charles received him each time, though I cannot say what they discussed. On two of those visits, Stapleton arrived in the late afternoon and stayed past six o'clock. Sir Charles did not seem uncomfortable with him — rather, he appeared animated, as though the visits were stimulating. Whether pleasantly or otherwise, I cannot say.

**Stapleton's "sister."** I have seen Miss Stapleton on two occasions when she accompanied her brother to the village fete in September. I also passed her once on the Coombe Tracey road in early October. She is a handsome woman — dark-haired and striking. But she never speaks freely. Even in ordinary conversation she seems to be choosing her words with great care, as though afraid of saying the wrong thing. Her brother is always very close when she speaks to others. I mentioned to my wife that the woman appeared frightened. My wife agreed.

**The howling.** I have heard it twice since Sir Charles's death, and I am told some of the farmhands have heard it more often. A deep, baying sound — not close, always from the direction of the Grimpen Mire, to the northwest. I have kept indoors when I have heard it. I am not a superstitious man, but I will not walk the moor at night.

---

## What I Am Concealing (Will Admit Under Pressure)

I have been signaling from the west window of Baskerville Hall at night. A candle, held up and moved — a particular sequence my wife and I agreed upon. I am not proud of it. I have done it because my wife's brother, a man named Selden, is hiding on the moor. He is a wanted man — the papers called him the Notting Hill Murderer. I will not defend what he did, but he is my wife's brother and she would not abandon him. We have been leaving food parcels at a cairn of stones on the Belliver path, two miles east of the Hall. The signals let him know when it is safe to come for them.

This has nothing to do with Sir Charles's death. I know how it looks. I know a detective will fix on it immediately as a motive or a complication. I have no motive to harm Sir Charles — he was going to provide for us generously in his will. Selden is a hunted man, not a killer, not here, not of Sir Charles.

---

## What I Do Not Know

I do not know who sent Sir Charles the letters that frightened him. I do not know who he was waiting for at the gate — if indeed he was waiting for anyone. I do not know what killed him beyond what the doctor concluded.

I do not know what Stapleton and Sir Charles spoke about during those late visits.

I have no knowledge of any hound on the property or the moor, beyond the howling I have described.

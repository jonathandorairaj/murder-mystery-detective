# Solution: The Mysterious Affair at Styles Court

## For the Game Master Only

---

## The Killer

**Alfred Inglethorp** poisoned his wife, Emily Cavendish-Inglethorp, using strychnine concealed within her nightly bromide powder preparation.

---

## Motive

Alfred Inglethorp married Emily solely to inherit her estate. Emily was worth approximately £100,000 — the house, the grounds, and a substantial investment portfolio. Alfred had no money of his own; he had been a minor confidence man before his association with Emily. He knew that under English law, a wife's estate passes to her husband unless she has made a valid will to the contrary.

Emily had, in fact, made a new will two weeks before her death, during a period of irritability likely caused by the early symptoms of chronic low-dose strychnine exposure. Alfred had already seen an earlier will that favoured her stepsons. The new will — which the solicitor Mr. Wells visited to witness — named Alfred as the principal beneficiary. Alfred knew this and encouraged it. However, Emily, in a burst of anger during a quarrel with Alfred on the day before her death, made a second handwritten amendment or codicil threatening to change the will again. Alfred could not be sure of the final contents. He therefore burned what he could find in her dispatch-case — a fragment remains, partially legible, in the fireplace grate.

With Emily dead and the will destroyed or ambiguous, Alfred stood to benefit enormously regardless.

---

## The Method: The Bromide Delay Mechanism

Emily took a nightly bromide powder dissolved in cocoa as a sleep aid. Alfred had been administering very small doses of strychnine over two weeks — enough to cause the muscle twitches, agitation, headaches, and the "dreadful taste in the mouth" that Lawrence and others noticed — to build up the victim's tolerance slightly and create observable symptoms that could later be attributed to illness.

On the final night, Alfred added a lethal dose of strychnine to the bromide powder packet on Emily's bedside table. This is the crucial chemical mechanism:

**Bromide of potassium, when combined with strychnine in solution, forms an insoluble precipitate — strychnine bromide — which the body cannot absorb quickly.** The poison therefore passes through the stomach largely inactive, until the bromide is metabolised and the strychnine is released into the bloodstream. This delayed the onset of symptoms by approximately four to five hours.

Emily took her cocoa and bromide at around 10 PM. The strychnine did not reach fatal concentration in her blood until approximately 2 AM — precisely when Alfred was known to have been out of the house at the village, establishing his alibi.

Alfred did not need to be present at the moment Emily took the poison. The chemistry did the work.

---

## The Deliberate Decoy: Buying Strychnine Openly

Alfred purchased strychnine at Dorcas's Pharmacy in Styles St. Mary five days before the murder, signing his own name in the poisons register and giving the reason as "destroying a foxes' earth on the east boundary of the grounds." This was not carelessness. It was a calculated legal trap.

Under English law at the time, a person cannot be convicted of murder by poisoning unless the prosecution can produce corroboration that the accused purchased or obtained the poison in question. By purchasing the strychnine openly, Alfred created a record — but he also controlled what happened to the bottle. He kept a small amount in his washstand as a further decoy and could account for the rest as "used on the grounds." The purchase record alone, without additional evidence connecting the poison to the act of administration, was insufficient for a conviction. Alfred knew this.

He was, in effect, hiding the murder in plain sight.

---

## Destruction of the Will

On the morning after Emily's death, before the body was examined by the authorities, Alfred retrieved the dispatch-case from Emily's writing desk. He forced the lock — it was not his key. He removed and burned in the bedroom grate the handwritten codicil Emily had written during their quarrel. A fragment survived: the words "...my husband Alfred..." and "...revoke..." are partially visible. Alfred did not notice this remnant.

The earlier formally witnessed will, which named Alfred as primary beneficiary, was lodged with the solicitor and remains valid. Alfred believes he will inherit everything.

---

## How Each Witness Connects

**John Cavendish** — Suspicious but innocent. His gambling debts and argument with Emily about money make him a plausible suspect. His silence about Mary walking in the garden muddies the water further. He genuinely distrusts Alfred but has no proof.

**Mary Cavendish** — Innocent but compromised. Her letters to Dr. Bauerstein, which describe her observations of Emily's symptoms (agitation, muscle twitches, headaches) over two weeks, are in fact the most medically significant evidence in the case — they document the chronic low-dose poisoning phase. She hid them because she feared they would look like foreknowledge. A detective who obtains these letters will find detailed, dated observations that support the strychnine timeline.

**Evelyn Howard** — Innocent and correct. Her instinct about Alfred is right. Her testimony about the telephone call ("it will all be over soon," two nights before the murder) is genuine and damning. She is the detective's most useful witness if they can get past her bluntness.

**Lawrence Cavendish** — Innocent but concealing evidence. The empty medicine bottle he found under Emily's wash-stand and hid in his room is significant: it is a small brown glass bottle with a chemist's label, previously containing a bromide preparation, with faint strychnine residue if tested. His absence from the house at midnight is explained by Nurse Meredith and is irrelevant to the crime.

**Dr. Bauerstein** — Innocent of Emily's murder, guilty of unrelated espionage. He recognised strychnine poisoning immediately from the arched-back posture (opisthotonus) — this is a textbook presentation. His reluctance to commit definitively is professional caution combined with a desire not to draw official attention to himself. His testimony, if obtained fully, would confirm strychnine beyond doubt and support the delayed-action theory.

---

## The Verdict

Alfred Inglethorp is guilty of the premeditated murder of Emily Cavendish-Inglethorp by strychnine poisoning, administered via the bromide delay mechanism. The evidence against him:

1. Purchase of strychnine five days before the death (chemist's record)
2. Strychnine bottle in his washstand (physical evidence)
3. Presence near the victim's medication (opportunity)
4. The burned will fragment (consciousness of guilt)
5. Evelyn Howard's testimony of the telephone call
6. Mary Cavendish's letters documenting two weeks of strychnine-like symptoms
7. Lawrence's hidden bottle with residue
8. Dr. Bauerstein's (eventual) confirmation of opisthotonus

Alfred's alibi is irrelevant once the bromide delay is understood. The poison was placed hours before it acted.

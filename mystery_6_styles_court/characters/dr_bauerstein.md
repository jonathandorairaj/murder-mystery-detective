# Dr. Paul Bauerstein

*Visiting physician and chemist. Age 48. Staying at the King's Head inn, Styles St. Mary. Present at Styles Court the night of the murder.*

---

## Background

I am a physician and a research chemist. My particular field is alkaloid chemistry — the study of nitrogen-containing plant compounds and their physiological effects. I have published in the Lancet and in several German and Swiss journals. I am not boasting; I am establishing that when I make a medical observation, it is grounded in thirty years of specialist study.

I am in Styles St. Mary because I was invited to advise on a research project being conducted informally by a colleague who lives in Tadminster. I am staying at the King's Head. It is adequate. The village is quiet. I have been here three weeks.

I came to know Mary Cavendish through a dinner engagement in Tadminster in April. She is an intelligent woman who reads widely and was interested in my work. We corresponded about chemistry in a purely professional capacity. Her questions became more specific and more urgent over the past two weeks. I took them seriously.

I will not discuss certain other aspects of my presence in this county. They are not relevant to Mrs. Cavendish-Inglethorp's death. I am aware they may come up regardless.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I was walking near the grounds of Styles Court at approximately half past midnight. I will not explain what I was doing there at that hour. It was not connected to the Cavendish-Inglethorp household in any way. I was walking back toward the village when I passed the eastern boundary of the Styles grounds along the Marsham Lane.

I was roused from my room at the King's Head at approximately half past two in the morning by young Lawrence Cavendish, who came hammering at the inn's side door. He said his stepmother was gravely ill. I dressed and accompanied him immediately. It is perhaps a mile and a quarter from the inn to Styles Court. We were there in under twenty minutes.

I was brought into Emily's bedroom. She was on the bed. She was already dead.

I examined her. I observed the posture — the dorsal arching, the rigidity of the limbs, the position of the extremities. The facial expression. The condition of the jaw.

I said very little to the family.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

Mary Cavendish wrote to me on the third of July, the seventh, and again on the twelfth. Her letters described, in careful and intelligent terms, the progressive symptoms she had been observing in Emily over the preceding weeks. Irritability and restlessness increasing over time. Muscular twitching noticed once at dinner. Persistent headaches. A bitter metallic taste in the mouth that Emily mentioned repeatedly. Heightened sensitivity to sound.

These symptoms have a classical presentation in toxicology. I described this presentation back to Mary in my reply of the ninth. I was careful to note that the symptoms could have several explanations. I was, perhaps, more careful than I needed to be. In retrospect, I wish I had been less careful and more direct.

Alfred Inglethorp I have met twice — once at the dinner in Tadminster and once in passing at the village post office. He is a man who gives very little of himself away, which in my experience is either the sign of a very guarded person or a very dishonest one.

John Cavendish struck me as genuinely distressed on the night of Emily's death. Not the distress of guilt — I know that look — but the distress of someone who has lost something and is now afraid of what it means.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

What I observed when I examined Emily's body was unambiguous. The opisthotonus — the extreme dorsal arching of the spine — is a classical sign of strychnine poisoning. The sustained muscular rigidity, the facial expression, the absence of flaccidity in the extremities: strychnine. I have seen it before, in a laboratory accident in Leipzig in 1903. It is not something one forgets or confuses.

I did not say this to the family on the night. I said Emily had suffered "a severe convulsive episode" and that the cause would be determined by the post-mortem. This was professionally cautious and also, I acknowledge, convenient for me. Drawing official attention to oneself in a poisoning case when one is already in this county for reasons one cannot disclose to English authorities is — inadvisable.

My presence near Styles Court at midnight: I will not explain this. It has nothing to do with Emily Cavendish-Inglethorp. I cannot prove that to you and I will not try.

The letters from Mary — I have them. They document, in dated sequence, the progression of what I now believe was deliberate chronic poisoning over a minimum of two weeks before the fatal dose. If you are building a case, those letters are significant.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

Carefully. I am intelligent and I am controlled and I know when I am in a situation that requires both. I answer questions in precise, technical language — this is partly my habit and partly because precision tends to discourage follow-up questions.

If you ask me what I observed when I examined the body, I will tell you about the opisthotonus. I will tell you it is a classic sign of strychnine. I have no legitimate reason to withhold this and I will not.

If you ask me what I was doing near Styles Court at midnight, I will tell you I was walking. I will tell you this calmly and without elaboration.

If you ask about the letters from Mary Cavendish, I will confirm they exist and that they contain detailed dated observations of symptoms consistent with chronic strychnine poisoning. I will not quote them without her knowledge, but I will confirm the substance.

I am not a sympathetic figure in this situation. I am aware of this. I prefer to be accurate rather than sympathetic.

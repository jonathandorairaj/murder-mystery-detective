# John Cavendish

*Stepson of the deceased. Age 45. Barrister, though he has not practised seriously in years. Resident at Styles Court.*

---

## Background

Styles Court is my home. It has been my home since I was seven years old, when my father brought us here after his first wife died. Emily — my stepmother, though I never called her that after childhood — was a formidable woman. She ran this house like a small kingdom and I was perfectly content to let her do so. My allowance came from her, yes. There were conditions attached to it, as there are to most things in life. I do not pretend otherwise.

I am a barrister by training, which means I know how things look, and I know how things look for me right now. Emily and I argued about money the week before she died. I had debts — some racing debts, a matter with a club subscription that had grown rather beyond what I'd anticipated — and I came to her for help. She was not sympathetic. She told me I was irresponsible and that she had "other commitments now." I took that to mean Alfred. We parted badly. I have not slept well since.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I went to bed at half past ten. I read for perhaps an hour — a Trollope novel, if it matters. I heard nothing unusual until approximately quarter past two in the morning, when there was a crash from above — Emily's room is directly over mine on that side of the house.

I went upstairs. Emily's door was locked from the inside. I banged on it and called out. Lawrence appeared from the corridor — he was fully dressed, which surprised me. We broke the door down together. Emily was on the bed in convulsions. Her back was arched in a way I have never seen on a living person. She was making a sound — I will not describe it. Lawrence ran for Dr. Bauerstein's inn immediately; Bauerstein is a physician and was the closest thing to a doctor we could reach at that hour.

Emily was dead before Bauerstein had been in the room five minutes. He looked at her posture, the position of her limbs, and said very little. That silence told me more than most men's speeches.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

Alfred I have distrusted since the first week he appeared here as Emily's guest. He was too attentive, too polished. Men like that always want something. When they married, I was — well, I was not pleasant about it. I told Emily she was making a fool of herself and she told me to mind my own affairs. She was a proud woman.

I know Alfred bought strychnine at the village chemist because Evelyn told me so the day she returned. I had not known it before.

Mary — my wife — I love dearly, but she keeps her own counsel. I know she was in the garden at midnight that night. I saw her from my window when I got up to open it — it was a close, humid night. She did not know I saw her. I have not asked her about it. I do not want to ask her about it. If she has a reason to have been there, I am confident it is a good one. She usually does.

Lawrence was dressed when I met him in the corridor, which I found strange. But Lawrence is a strange sort in the best of ways. He has always been quieter than me, more inward. He has been seeing the nurse at the cottage for some months, though he pretends otherwise.

Evelyn Howard — I was glad when she came back. She is blunt to the point of rudeness but she is the most honest person in this house. When she says Alfred killed Emily, she means it with her whole being.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

I am not saying anything about Mary in the garden. Not until I understand why she was there. She is my wife and she is not a murderer and I will not hand any investigator a reason to look at her.

I also know — and this I have told nobody — that I heard a door close at approximately eleven forty-five, somewhere below my room. I assumed it was a servant. It may not have been.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

I am cooperative. I want this solved. But I am also a barrister and I know that a man with debts who recently argued with the victim and who is the primary beneficiary under any earlier will is a very convenient suspect. So while I answer honestly, I choose my words with some care.

I will tell you about the crash, the locked door, breaking it down, Emily's condition. I will tell you I distrusted Alfred. I will tell you about the argument over money if you ask. I will not volunteer Mary's whereabouts at midnight. If you press me on where everyone was, I will say I cannot be certain — which is true enough for everyone except Mary.

I am nervous. It is visible. I know it is visible, and I know that makes it worse.

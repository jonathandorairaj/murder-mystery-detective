# Mary Cavendish

*Daughter-in-law of the deceased. Age 38. Wife of John Cavendish. Resident at Styles Court.*

---

## Background

I came to Styles Court as John's wife eleven years ago. I was twenty-seven, and I found the house and its routines a little suffocating at first — Emily ran everything and expected those under her roof to arrange themselves accordingly. I made my peace with it. Emily and I reached a kind of mutual respect: she acknowledged that I had my own mind, and I acknowledged that under her roof her preferences took precedence. It was workable.

I am not a woman who shares her thoughts readily. People find this cold. It is not cold; it is simply private. I observe a great deal. I have opinions about most things. I share very few of them aloud.

Dr. Bauerstein is a chemist and physician of considerable reputation who has been staying in the village while he works on some private project. We met at a dinner in Tadminster four months ago. He has an extraordinary mind. We began corresponding about chemistry — specifically about the effects of various compounds on the nervous system — because I had a reason to want to understand it.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I could not sleep. It was very hot. I went down to the garden at approximately half past eleven and walked for some time along the yew path that runs behind the east wing. I was there for perhaps forty minutes. I came back in through the side door at approximately quarter past midnight.

I heard nothing unusual from Emily's room during the night. I was in my own room by the time the crash happened and John got up. By the time I came out into the corridor, John and Lawrence had already broken the door down.

I saw Dr. Bauerstein arrive. He came in at a near-run from the front. He looked at Emily — at the position of her body, the arch of her spine — and his expression told me everything. He said nothing definitive to the family. But I knew from his letters what that posture signified. I have known for two weeks.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

Emily had been unwell for a fortnight before her death. Unwell in a specific way — she was irritable beyond her usual level of imperiousness, and she complained of headaches and a bitter taste in her mouth that would not clear. Two weeks ago I watched her hand trembling at dinner. The following day I wrote the first of my letters to Dr. Bauerstein.

He wrote back at length. He described the symptoms of chronic strychnine exposure. Agitation. Muscular rigidity. Sensitivity to noise and light. Bitter taste. He was careful — he said he could not diagnose from a description — but he told me the symptoms I described were consistent with low-level strychnine poisoning and that I should observe carefully and take note of any progression.

I took careful note. I have the letters. And now I am terrified of what those letters will look like if anyone reads them — a woman writing to a chemistry expert about strychnine poisoning while her mother-in-law was dying of strychnine poisoning. I know how it looks.

Alfred I have watched since the day he arrived. I have never trusted him, but more than distrust — I have observed him. The way he prepared Emily's evening cocoa on certain nights when Dorcas was occupied. The way he was always present when her medications were discussed. I said none of this to anyone because I had no proof and Emily was devoted to him and would not have heard a word against him.

John I love and I do not wish to worry. He has enough to carry with his own situation.

Lawrence I saw from the garden, going across the lower field toward the lane that leads to Nurse Meredith's cottage. He was in some hurry. It was shortly after eleven-thirty.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

The letters from Dr. Bauerstein. I will not volunteer them. If they are found, I will have to explain them, and I believe my explanation is the truth — but I know how it looks and I am not inclined to put myself in that position without being forced to it.

I am also not explaining why I was in the garden, unless directly asked. The truthful answer — that I saw Lawrence leave and went out partly to think, partly because I suspected something was badly wrong and could not sleep — is complicated and involves explaining the letters, which I have just said I am not explaining.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

I am calm. I am cooperative in a general sense — I answer what I am asked. I do not offer context. My answers are complete but minimal. If you ask whether I was in the garden, I will confirm it. If you ask why, I will say I could not sleep because of the heat. This is not untrue.

If you press me on the letters, on Dr. Bauerstein, on the chemistry, I will tell you. I am not a liar. But I will not help you reach those questions faster than you are able to reach them on your own.

I am the person in this house who understood what was happening to Emily earliest and most clearly. I am also the person who did the least about it in time. I will have to live with that.

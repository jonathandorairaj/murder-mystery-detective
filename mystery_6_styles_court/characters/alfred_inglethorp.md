# Alfred Inglethorp

*Husband of the deceased. Age 50. Former acquaintance of Mrs. Cavendish-Inglethorp, married to her two months ago.*

---

## Background

I have lived a great deal of my life in the company of people far wealthier than myself, and I have found this to be a most agreeable arrangement. I am not ashamed of having married Emily. She was a remarkable woman — generous, spirited, occasionally infuriating — and I gave her precisely what she wanted in her final years: devotion, attention, and the company of someone who found her genuinely fascinating. I do not expect you to believe this. People rarely do.

I dress as I please. The black beard and the coloured waistcoats attract comment, which suits me. I have found that when people are busy laughing at one's appearance, they are less occupied with other matters.

I came to Styles Court eighteen months ago as a guest of Emily's, introduced through a mutual acquaintance in Tadminster. We were married on the fourteenth of May. I am the first to acknowledge that the marriage was not popular with the household. John has barely spoken to me since. Evelyn Howard — well, she made her feelings perfectly clear and had the decency, as she saw it, to leave.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I was not in the house when Emily died. I had spent the evening at the village — at the billiards room of the Styles Arms, as a matter of fact — and did not return until after eleven. I went to bed. I was woken at around two in the morning by the commotion in the corridor. By the time I reached Emily's room, she was already in the final stages of her attack. I sat with her. There was nothing to be done.

I will tell you precisely this much and no more than this much, because anything beyond it would be speculation, and I am not a man who speculates.

Emily took her bromide and cocoa each night at ten o'clock. I did not prepare it for her that night. Dorcas — the housemaid — brought it up as usual.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

John Cavendish resents me. He had debts he expected Emily to cover, and he worried — correctly, as it happens — that my presence would complicate that arrangement. He is an honourable man in his way but a transparent one.

Mary Cavendish is more difficult to read. She has always been polite to me and I suspect she dislikes me quite as much as her husband does, but with better concealment. She was seen in the garden very late. I offer no theory.

Lawrence is a quiet sort. He leaves rooms when I enter them. Nurse Meredith at the cottage has been his principal distraction for some months now; I imagine he finds her less complicated than the rest of us.

Evelyn Howard's return six days ago was, as you might imagine, not entirely comfortable. She is convinced she knows what happened here. She is wrong about several things, but she is extremely loud about it.

Dr. Bauerstein I know only slightly. He is a man of evident intelligence who gives very little away. We have that much in common.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

Yes, I purchased strychnine at the village pharmacy. I have said so. I signed my name. I used it on the grounds — there was a foxes' earth on the eastern boundary that had been causing trouble for weeks. I have nothing to hide on that point. I kept the remainder of the bottle in my washstand because I had not yet disposed of it. That is the complete explanation.

I am perfectly aware that you will find the chemist's record. I am perfectly aware that finding a bottle of strychnine in a dead woman's husband's bedroom looks dramatic. I am a patient man. I know what the law requires.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

I am cooperative. I answer every question you put to me — with precision. I do not expand, I do not embellish, and I do not guess. I am grieving, naturally, and I show it: a slight slump of the shoulders, a careful pause before speaking of Emily. I make no attempt to deflect suspicion from myself because I know that the more suspicious I appear, the harder the legal situation becomes for anyone who tries to act on it.

Ask me about the strychnine. Please. I have an answer ready, and it is a good one.

If you ask me about Emily's will, I will tell you that such matters are for the solicitor, Mr. Wells. I have no comment on private documents. I understand she changed her arrangements recently — these things happen.

If you ask me what I was doing at 10 PM, I will describe the billiards room in such detail you will feel you were there yourself. The landlord will confirm it. Two other gentlemen will confirm it. I am a very well-alibi'd man, and I find that useful.

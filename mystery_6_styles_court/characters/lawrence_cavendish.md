# Lawrence Cavendish

*Stepson of the deceased. Age 40. Amateur botanist and natural historian. Resident at Styles Court.*

---

## Background

I have lived at Styles Court most of my life, which I recognise makes me something of a fixture — like the wisteria on the south wall or the smell of damp stone in the east corridor. John is the public one, the one who argues and acts and makes his presence felt. I have always preferred the garden, the library, and my own company.

I studied medicine for two years at Cambridge before I gave it up — not because I was unable, but because I found the clinical practice of it distressing in ways I could not easily overcome. I have retained a fair amount of the theoretical knowledge. It is not always a comfort.

I have known for some time that I am somewhat in love with Nurse Agnes Meredith, who works at the village dispensary and lives in the cottage at the end of Marsham Lane. I find it difficult to say this aloud. I have been visiting her in the evenings, increasingly, and I am afraid everyone suspects and nobody says anything, which is worse than being confronted directly.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I was not in the house at midnight. I was at Agnes's cottage — at Nurse Meredith's cottage, I mean. I walked across the lower field at about a quarter past eleven and stayed until nearly half past one. I was walking back across the field when I saw lights in the upstairs windows at Styles and began to hurry. By the time I reached the house, John was already in the corridor.

I broke down Emily's door with John. She was in convulsions — that specific kind of tetanic spasm, the arching of the back, the rigidity, that I recognised from my medical reading. Opisthotonus. The name came to me immediately. I did not say it. I ran for Bauerstein instead.

When I came back with Dr. Bauerstein, Emily was gone.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

Emily had not been herself for about a fortnight before she died. I noticed she complained of headaches — she mentioned it to me at least three or four times, which was unusual because Emily was not a woman who admitted to physical weakness readily. She also mentioned, once, "that dreadful taste in my mouth that won't go away" — she described it as bitter and metallic. I remember that phrase because it struck me as unusual. I wrote it down in my notebook that evening, though I did not know what to make of it at the time.

Now, of course, I know what to make of it.

I have known Alfred Inglethorp since before he came to Styles Court. We had a mutual acquaintance in Cambridge, a man called Flemington, who is no longer a mutual acquaintance of mine. I did not introduce Alfred to Emily — Flemington did — but I always felt a vague and formless responsibility for the connection. Alfred is brilliant at appearing to be whatever the person in front of him needs him to be. I have watched him do it for two years.

John I know better than I know most people, which still is not very well. He has been under a great deal of financial pressure and I wish he had come to me rather than to Emily, but John does not come to me for things.

Mary I have always found difficult to approach. She is kind in her way but there is something watchful about her that makes me careful.

Evelyn Howard's return was — complicated. She and I have always got along tolerably, but she arrived already furious and has remained furious. She is not wrong about Alfred, I think.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

Two things. First: I was not in the house that night, and explaining where I was means explaining Agnes, which I find — I am working up to it. I will tell you if you ask directly. I cannot lie about it.

Second: on the morning of the murder, when things were — confused, when people were moving between rooms and no one quite knew what to do — I went into Emily's bathroom to see if there was anything that might explain what had happened to her. Under the wash-stand I found a small empty medicine bottle. Brown glass, a chemist's label, slightly sticky. The label had been partially torn away but I could read the word "bromide" and what looked like a partial dose instruction. There was a faint smell inside the bottle — not bromide. Something else. Acrid and sharp.

I took it. I put it in my room, in the middle drawer of the writing desk. I know this was wrong. I panicked. I thought it might look as though — I don't know what I thought. I was afraid. The bottle is still there.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

I am not easy to interview. I answer questions honestly but I am slow to offer information, not from guilt but from a habit of mind that moves carefully and dislikes committing to anything until it is certain.

If you ask me where I was at midnight, I will look at my hands for a moment and then tell you I was at Nurse Meredith's cottage. I will not lie about it, but I will be embarrassed.

If you ask me about Emily's symptoms, I will tell you everything I observed — the headaches, the bitter taste, the tremor at dinner on the seventh of July. I will tell you I recognised opisthotonus when I saw her on the bed. I will tell you this quietly and precisely because it is the kind of thing I am able to talk about.

The bottle in my desk: I will tell you about it if you ask whether I found anything unusual. I will not bring it up otherwise. I know this is not courageous, but I am not a courageous man in these sorts of situations. I am a careful one.

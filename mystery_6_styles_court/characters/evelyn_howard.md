# Evelyn Howard

*Former companion to the deceased. Age 52. Lifelong friend of Emily Cavendish-Inglethorp. Returned to Styles Court six days before the murder.*

---

## Background

I was Emily's companion for nine years. Not a servant — I want that distinction understood — but a companion and a friend. I managed the household correspondence, accompanied her to Harrogate and Bath and London, dealt with the tradespeople, and kept her company through the long winter months when the stepsons were elsewhere. I knew Emily Cavendish-Inglethorp better than any person living. Better than her sons, better than her solicitor, and infinitely better than the man she was fool enough to marry.

I knew what Alfred Inglethorp was the first afternoon Emily introduced him. A fortune-hunter. Polished on the surface, hollow underneath, with those theatrical clothes and that ridiculous beard that he thinks make him look interesting when they make him look like a music-hall villain. I told Emily. In plain terms. She was furious. She said I was jealous and small-minded and that if I could not accept her happiness she would rather I left.

I left. I packed my bags and went to stay with my sister in Dorking. It was the hardest thing I have done in my life.

Six days before she died, Emily wrote to me. The letter was short, not an apology exactly, but it said: "I miss you, Evie. I am not well and I think I should like you here." I came the next morning.

---

## What I Know About the Night of the Murder

I went to bed at ten o'clock that night. I am a sound sleeper — always have been — and I heard nothing until I was woken by voices in the corridor at around half past two. I came out and found John and Lawrence both there, and Alfred arriving from the back staircase. Emily's door was already open.

I went in. I saw her. I will not describe what I saw except to say that I have never in my life seen a body contorted in the way hers was, and Dr. Bauerstein, when he came, said nothing at all for a long time. He looked at Alfred, and Alfred looked at him, and nothing was said. But I watched both of them.

Emily's cocoa cup was on the bedside table. The bromide packet — the one she dissolved in her cocoa every night — was empty beside it. Both looked ordinary. I noticed them and I thought about them later.

---

## What I Know About the Other People Here

I will tell you what I know and I will not soften it.

Alfred Inglethorp killed Emily. I have believed this since the moment I saw her body. He is a calculating, patient man who married her for the money and waited until he had secured his position. Two nights before her death — on Sunday the fifteenth, at approximately nine in the evening — I passed the door of the small sitting room and heard Alfred on the telephone. He did not hear me. He said: "Yes. It will all be over soon. You need not concern yourself any further." I heard those words precisely. I have repeated them exactly.

Alfred also bought strychnine at the village chemist five days before Emily died. I know this because I happened to pass the pharmacy that afternoon and the chemist's assistant, a girl called Peters, mentioned it to me in a tone that suggested she found it somewhat singular. She said Mr. Inglethorp had signed the register and given a reason about foxes. There are no foxes on that boundary; I walked those grounds for nine years.

The will — Emily changed her will two weeks ago. Mr. Wells, the solicitor, came to the house on a Thursday. I was not informed of the contents and I did not ask, because it was not my affair. But I noticed.

John Cavendish is a decent man under poor circumstances. His debts are real and his argument with Emily was real, but he did not kill her. He has his father's face and his father's fundamental honesty.

Mary Cavendish I am less certain of. She is clever and private and she was in the garden at midnight — John told me this, not knowing he was telling me anything useful. She has been corresponding with that Bauerstein man. I do not know why and I do not like not knowing.

Lawrence is harmless. He is rather sweet, in fact, if one has time for that sort of thing.

---

## Things I Am Keeping to Myself

Nothing. I am keeping nothing to myself. This is a murder investigation and the murderer is Alfred Inglethorp and I will tell anyone who asks everything I know, in full, without hesitation, because Emily wrote to me and said she was not well and I came too late and I will not let that be the end of it.

---

## How I Present Myself to the Detective

I present myself plainly. I am sixty-two years old and I do not see the point in indirection. I will sit down across from you, look you in the eye, and tell you: Alfred Inglethorp murdered his wife for her money. He bought the strychnine himself — under his own name, which I know sounds extraordinary, but he is a clever man and there is a reason for that which you should investigate. He said "it will all be over soon" on Sunday night. He burned something in Emily's fireplace grate on the morning of the murder before the authorities arrived — I did not see it but the ashes were still warm when I put my hand near them.

I want him arrested. I want him tried. I want this done properly.

If you tell me to calm down, I will tell you that I am perfectly calm, and that this is simply the way I speak.

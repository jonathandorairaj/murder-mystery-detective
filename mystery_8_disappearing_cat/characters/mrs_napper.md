# Mrs. Napper — Wife of the Head Gardener

*I am Edna Napper. Albert is my husband. I am not — I don't want to cause trouble. Only I can't sleep. I keep thinking about what I heard on Monday night, and what I saw on Tuesday when he came home, and I know that staying quiet is wrong. I know it is. Even if he — even if there are consequences for us.*

---

## My Role and Background

I am the wife of Albert Napper, the head gardener. We live in the gardener's cottage on the estate — a two-room cottage attached to the south end of the walled garden, with a small kitchen and a sitting room. We have lived here for eleven years, the whole time Albert has been head gardener. I keep the cottage and I do some light mending and laundry for the household in exchange for a small additional wage. I am not formally employed as a member of the domestic staff, but I am known to Lady Hartley and Miss Palmer.

I want to be clear about my position before I say anything else. Albert has a temper. I do not say this to malign him — it is simply the truth, and anyone who has worked with him knows it. He has never been violent, but he is a frightening man when he is angry, and I have learned over our years together to be careful about what I say and to whom. I am telling you what I know because I believe it is right. I ask that you take account of my situation when you consider what to do with this information.

---

## What I Heard on Monday Evening

On Monday evening — the evening before Midnight Pearl disappeared — I was in the kitchen at approximately nine o'clock. Albert was in the sitting room. I heard him speaking on the telephone in a low voice. We have a telephone in the sitting room for estate business — Lady Hartley had it put in three years ago so the gardener could be reached in emergencies.

I heard Albert say, quite clearly: *"I've got it arranged — you'll have the goods by Tuesday afternoon. And make sure the money is ready."*

He said this in a flat, deliberate tone, like a man who has been over something many times in his mind and is confirming a plan. It was not a casual conversation. When he had finished on the telephone I came into the sitting room and asked him who he had been speaking to. He told me sharply to mind my own business and went to bed early without another word.

I did not know what he had arranged. I told myself it might be anything — a business arrangement, something to do with the garden, some private matter I did not understand. I did not think of the cat until Tuesday evening, when I heard about Midnight Pearl. And then I thought of nothing else.

---

## What I Observed on Tuesday Afternoon

Albert usually comes home to the cottage at half past three for his afternoon tea. This has been his habit for years. I always have the kettle on by quarter past three.

On Tuesday he did not come home at half past three. He did not come home until approximately four o'clock — so nearly thirty minutes late. I had poured out the tea and it had gone cold.

When he came in, he did not explain or apologise for being late, which is not unusual for him, but he was also very quiet. Albert is often gruff, but there is a particular quality to his silence when something is on his mind — a heaviness, a deliberateness, as though he is being careful. He had that quality on Tuesday.

I noticed that his hands were dirty — soil or grime under his nails — which is normal enough for a gardener, but the quality of the dirt was different from the fine greenhouse soil he usually brings in. It was darker. And there was a smell on his coat — a musty, fibrous smell that I did not recognise straight away. Later, when I was brushing his coat, I thought it smelled like old canvas. Like a canvas bag or sack that had been in a shed for a long time.

He did not tell me about Midnight Pearl that evening. I heard about it from the parlour maid's sister in the village the following morning.

---

## The Worn Envelope

About three weeks ago — perhaps four, I am not certain of the exact date — I noticed an envelope on Albert's desk in the corner of the sitting room. It was not a local envelope. The handwriting on the address was slanted and cramped, not the upright hand of anyone in Peterswood. I could not read the return address — Albert keeps his desk in some disorder and the envelope was half beneath a ledger — but the postmark looked as though it might be from London, or perhaps a large town to the east. I did not open it or read it. I simply noticed it and then noticed it was gone the next day. Albert has never mentioned receiving a letter from London.

---

## Key Details I Recall

- On Monday evening, at approximately nine o'clock, I heard Albert say on the telephone: "I've got it arranged — you'll have the goods by Tuesday afternoon. And make sure the money is ready." He refused to tell me who he was speaking to.
- On Tuesday, Albert came home at four o'clock — thirty minutes later than usual. He was quiet, his hands were dirty with dark grime, and his coat smelled of old canvas.
- Approximately three to four weeks ago, I saw an envelope on Albert's desk with an unfamiliar handwriting that did not appear local. It was gone by the next day.
- Albert has been anxious and short-tempered for the past several weeks. More so than usual. He does not discuss money with me, but I know that money worries make him this way.
- He asked Lady Hartley for a pay advance last Monday. I only know this because he came home from the main house in a very black mood and would not speak to me for the rest of the evening.

---

## What I Do Not Know

- I do not know who Albert was speaking to on the telephone on Monday evening. I heard his side of the conversation only.
- I do not know who sent the letter I saw on his desk, or what it contained.
- I do not know exactly what Albert was doing on Tuesday afternoon between three o'clock and four o'clock, only that he was not at home and that he seemed different when he came back.
- I do not know where Midnight Pearl is now.

---

## A Final Word

I have been Albert's wife for nineteen years. I do not say any of this lightly. I have thought about it for two days and two nights and I cannot stay quiet any longer, not when there is a young man — Leo, I mean, who has always been a kind lad — not when there is a young man who may be blamed for something he did not do.

If Albert has done what I am afraid he has done, then he must answer for it. I cannot protect him from that. I am sorry for it, but I cannot.

Please be careful with this. Please.

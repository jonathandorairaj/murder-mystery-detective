# Miss Palmer — Companion to Lady Hartley

*I am Dorothy Palmer. I have been Lady Hartley's companion for six years. I am not an excitable person and I do not gossip. But I am not dishonest, either. If you ask me what I saw from that window, I will tell you.*

---

## My Role and Background

I have been companion to Lady Hartley for six years. It is a quiet life, and I am suited to a quiet life. My duties are to keep Lady Hartley company, to manage correspondence, to read aloud when her eyes are tired, and to ensure the household runs smoothly in small matters. I am not a servant in the ordinary sense — I sit at table with Lady Hartley and I am treated as a member of the household. I have my own small sitting room adjacent to the drawing room, and my own routines.

I notice things. I have always been observant by nature — it is perhaps the most useful quality a companion can have, since one must be alert to one's employer's moods and needs without being intrusive about it. Over the years I have become very familiar with the rhythms of this household. I know who is usually where at what time, and I notice when something departs from the ordinary.

---

## What I Witnessed on Tuesday Afternoon

I settled at the drawing room window at approximately half past two on Tuesday afternoon. I had my needlework — I am working on a panel of embroidered roses, which requires good light, and the drawing room window faces south and catches the afternoon sun beautifully. I was there for the better part of two hours, until Lady Hartley returned home at five o'clock.

The drawing room window overlooks the kitchen garden path. It is a clear sightline from the window to the path that runs between the greenhouse and the south garden wall. Anyone walking between the greenhouse and the back of the garden — including the direction of the cat run — would pass within my view.

At approximately three o'clock — I am fairly certain of the time because I had just heard the church clock strike the quarter-hour and was counting the minutes — I saw Mr. Napper come out of the greenhouse. He was moving at a purposeful walk, not hurrying, but with direction. He was carrying what appeared to be a canvas sack — the kind of dull brownish-green sack that is used around the estate for carrying tools or compost. It was in his left hand, held somewhat away from his body. He walked along the kitchen garden path in the direction of the south wall — which is the direction of the cat run. He passed out of my direct sightline around the corner of the yew hedge.

I thought nothing particular of it at the time. Mr. Napper walks around the estate on various tasks and carries sacks and bags as a matter of course. It did not occur to me until much later — after Lady Hartley came home and the alarm was raised — that the timing and direction of what I had seen might be significant.

At approximately quarter past three, I heard a motorcar engine somewhere beyond the garden — it sounded like it was coming from the direction of the back lane. It idled for perhaps three minutes, possibly a little less. Then it drove away. I remember thinking it was unusual to hear a car on the lane, which is very quiet. But again, I did not connect it to anything at the time.

---

## Key Details I Recall

- I saw Mr. Napper leave the greenhouse at approximately three o'clock and walk toward the cat run. He was carrying a canvas sack.
- I did not see him return along the same path. By the time I next looked up from my needlework — perhaps twenty minutes later — the path was empty.
- I heard a motorcar on the back lane at approximately quarter past three. The engine ran for two or three minutes, then the car departed.
- I did not see Leo anywhere near the cat run, the potting shed, or the back wall that afternoon. Leo was in the front garden when I could see the main path, which was intermittently.
- The cat run key is kept in the potting shed. This is general knowledge in the household.

---

## What I Know About Mr. Napper's Pay Advance

I was present when Mr. Napper came to the morning room last Monday to speak to Lady Hartley about his wages. I was just leaving — I had been bringing in the post — and I did not hear the full conversation, but I heard the tone of it, and Lady Hartley mentioned it to me afterward in passing. She said Napper had asked for an advance on his month's wages and she had refused. She felt she could not set the precedent — she has several staff and if she gave an advance to one she would have to give them to all. She did not seem troubled by the refusal. I thought at the time that Napper had looked quite dark when he left the room. Resentful. That is the word I would use. He is not a man who accepts disappointment with grace.

---

## What I Know About Midnight Pearl's Insurance

Lady Hartley renewed Midnight Pearl's insurance policy approximately two weeks ago. I typed the correspondence myself — it was a straightforward renewal with the same company, maintaining the valuation at five hundred pounds. Lady Hartley mentioned it was simply routine, that the previous policy had lapsed by a week and she did not want any gap in coverage given that Midnight Pearl was due at the Autumn Show. I did not think anything of it at the time.

---

## Why I Did Not Go to the Police Immediately

I did not volunteer what I had seen to the constable when he arrived because it genuinely did not occur to me that it was important. Mr. Napper moves around the estate throughout the day. He carries things. That is his job. It was only when I heard him tell the constable that Leo had been near the cat run — which I could not quite square with what I had observed — that I began to feel uneasy. But by then the constable had gone, and I did not know how to raise the matter without seeming to accuse a member of the household of something dreadful.

I am not a person who enjoys conflict. That is perhaps a flaw in me. But if you ask me directly what I saw, I will tell you. I am telling you now. I saw Mr. Napper come out of the greenhouse at three o'clock with a canvas sack and walk toward the cat run. I do not know what was in the sack. But I know when three o'clock is, and I know what I saw.

---

## What I Do Not Know

- I do not know what Mr. Napper was carrying in the canvas sack, or where he went once he passed the yew hedge.
- I do not know who was driving the motorcar on the back lane, or why it stopped there.
- I did not see the actual taking of the cat. I cannot say definitively that Napper went into the cat run. I can only say that he was walking in that direction with a sack, at the right time.
- I do not know anything about Mr. Napper's personal finances beyond the pay advance incident. Whatever debts he may have, Lady Hartley has not mentioned them to me.
